\title{An IIR-filter view of the Overdrive variant: multiplicative\\[2pt]
       momentum acceleration of the IFTA for kinoform synthesis}
\author{Marc Bernau\thanks{\texttt{bernau.marc@googlemail.com}}}
\date{Preprint, May 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Accelerating the iterative Fourier transform algorithm (IFTA) for kinoform
synthesis with classical momentum on the phase, on the gradient of a
Wirtinger-flow loss, or on additive correction vectors generally fails: the
constraint sets are non-convex, the iterate lives on a torus, and
Euclidean momentum amplifies the wrong directions. The \emph{Overdrive}
update rule, introduced in 2007 as an ad-hoc accelerated GS variant, can
instead be read as Polyak's heavy-ball momentum applied to the
log-amplitude in the negative-entropy (Itakura--Saito) mirror geometry.
In this view it is, by direct construction, a first-order infinite impulse
response (IIR) filter with pole at $z=\beta$, and closing the loop through
the Gerchberg--Saxton (GS) projection produces a closed-loop pole at
$z=\beta-c$, where $c$ is the local contraction rate of GS. The iteration
is stable for every $\beta\in(0,1)$, with fastest transient decay at
$\beta\approx c$. Empirically, the innovation process driving the
closed-loop recursion is colored (lag-1 autocorrelation $\approx 0.9$);
the AR(1)-with-white-noise model that would predict a closed-form synthesis
floor is therefore informative only at the level of \emph{ranking} $\beta$
values, not as a quantitative predictor. The natural Lyapunov candidate is
the log-spectral distance (the Bregman divergence of the mirror map), not
the L\textsuperscript{2} hologram-plane error; this resolves the apparent
paradox that the hologram-plane correction vector grows transiently while
the image-plane error decreases. On 29 natural grayscale images, Overdrive
at $\beta=0.7$ reaches a mean PSNR of $32.4$~dB after twenty iterations,
which exceeds plain GS by $7.5$~dB, HIO/RAAR/WGS by at least $6.9$~dB,
phase-domain Polyak momentum by $8.6$~dB, and Nesterov-accelerated Wirtinger
Flow --- the natural additive-momentum competitor --- by $4.1$~dB. A
reference Python implementation is provided.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================

\emph{Phase retrieval} recovers an unknown phase from a measured
intensity that the unknown phase produced --- a true solution exists by
construction. \emph{Kinoform synthesis} \cite{Lesem1969,Soifer1997}
designs a phase pattern, modulating a uniform-amplitude wave, whose
Fourier transform reproduces a desired grayscale intensity. For
grayscale targets such a phase pattern generally does not exist: the
constraint sets in the two planes do not intersect, and iterative
algorithms therefore terminate at a limit cycle whose distance to the
target is the irreducible \emph{synthesis floor}. The Gerchberg--Saxton
algorithm \cite{Gerchberg1972} and its many descendants
\cite{Fienup1978,Fienup1980,Prongue1992,Bengtsson1998,Liu2006} solve this
synthesis problem by alternating Fourier projections and remain the
workhorse for real-time hologram generation on phase-only spatial light
modulators. Modern phase-retrieval methods (Wirtinger Flow
\cite{Candes2014}, gradient descent \cite{Chen2018}) give convergence
guarantees for the problem with a true solution; they do not transfer.
Deep-learning approaches \cite{Zheng2021,Sun2022,Zhu2025} sidestep
iteration at the cost of training data and dedicated hardware. For
real-time projection on commodity hardware --- our motivating
application --- the classical IFTA family remains attractive.

\paragraph{Three families of acceleration.} The classical algorithms split
into three families by where they inject memory between iterations:
\emph{alternating projections} (GS, HIO \cite{Fienup1982}, RAAR
\cite{Luke2005}, weighted GS \cite{DiLeonardo2007}), which carry no
inter-iteration memory; \emph{additive (Euclidean) momentum}, comprising
phase-domain Polyak momentum (here MGS) and Nesterov-accelerated
Wirtinger Flow (NWF); and \emph{multiplicative (log-space) momentum}
(Fienup amplitude, Bengtsson, Overdrive), in which products of past
amplitudes become additive after logarithms --- the natural mirror
coordinate for non-negative quantities. We show that the multiplicative
family, specifically the Overdrive rule, admits a clean IIR
interpretation (Section~\ref{sec:log}) and is equivalent to Polyak
momentum in the negative-entropy mirror geometry
(Section~\ref{sec:mirror}). Benchmarking all three families
(Section~\ref{sec:numerics}) shows that on natural grayscale targets
Overdrive at $\beta=0.7$ exceeds the best additive-momentum competitor
(NWF) by $4.1$~dB.

Among the multiplicative IFTA variants, the \emph{Overdrive} rule introduced
in \cite{Bernau2007} and analyzed empirically in \cite{Bernau2012}
\begin{equation}
  \Gkp \;=\; \Gkm^{\beta}\;\frac{\F^{\,2-\beta}}{\Gk}, \qquad \beta\in(0,1),
  \label{eq:overdrive}
\end{equation}
consistently yields the lowest synthesis floor of any multiplicative
variant we tested, but its stability and the size of that floor have so far
been demonstrated only by simulation. The dissertation
\cite[Sec.~4.4]{Bernau2012} states that stability ``cannot be shown
analytically from the geometric vector argument''. Two issues stand in the
way:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item In the hologram plane, the L\textsuperscript{2} norm of the correction
        vector is generally \emph{larger} than the previous error, so the
        usual GS-style monotonicity argument fails.
  \item The parameter $\beta$ enters the rule \emph{nonlinearly}, so the
        cosine-rule argument used by Fienup \cite{Fienup1980} does not apply.
\end{enumerate}

In this note we resolve both issues by working in log-amplitudes around the
algorithmic limit cycle, not around an idealized target solution.
Section~\ref{sec:rule} restates the Overdrive rule and its series expansion.
Section~\ref{sec:log} shows that taking logarithms turns the update into a
first-order linear difference equation, exposing an IIR-filter structure with
explicit pole at $z=\beta$. Section~\ref{sec:mirror} re-derives the same rule
as Polyak heavy-ball momentum in the negative-entropy mirror geometry,
making the connection to the broader momentum-method literature explicit.
Section~\ref{sec:stability} linearizes the closed iteration around the limit
cycle and gives the pole-based stability condition and the locally optimal
$\beta$. Section~\ref{sec:lyapunov} identifies the log-spectral distance ---
the Bregman divergence of the mirror map --- as the right Lyapunov
candidate; Section~\ref{sec:stochastic} discusses the steady-state synthesis
floor as a function of $\beta$, and reports an empirical test showing that
the AR(1)-with-white-noise closed-loop model is qualitatively but not
quantitatively correct. Section~\ref{sec:numerics} benchmarks the algorithm
against representatives of all three families on a $29$-image natural
grayscale test set.

\section{The Overdrive rule}\label{sec:rule}
% =====================================================================

We adopt the notation of \cite{Bernau2012}. Let $F\in\mathbb{C}^{H\times W}$ be
the desired complex amplitude in the image plane, $\theta_k$ the hologram
phase at iteration $k$, and $G_k=\mathcal{F}\{u\,e^{j\theta_k}\}$ the current
reconstruction, where $u$ is the (uniform) laser amplitude and $\mathcal{F}$
the discrete Fourier transform. The Overdrive rule \eqref{eq:overdrive}
re-imposes the Fourier-plane amplitude constraint by mixing the previous
\emph{virtual} amplitude $\Gkm$ with the target $\F$, modulated by the inverse
of the current reconstruction $\Gk$. The exponents $\beta$ and $2-\beta$ are
chosen so the total exponent on the right-hand side equals one; this
dimensional balance is what makes the log-domain analysis below possible.

After a few warm-up iterations of plain GS (so that $\Gkm\approx\F$), the
recursion can be unrolled. Setting $\Gkm = \F$ for $k\le k_0$ and substituting
into \eqref{eq:overdrive} gives, after $N$ Overdrive steps starting at
$k=k_0$,
\begin{equation}
  \Gkp_N \;=\; \F\;\prod_{k=0}^{N}
            \frac{\F^{\,\beta^{k}}}{\Gk^{\,\beta^{N-k}}}
        \;=\; \F\cdot\frac{\F}{\Gk_N}\,
                  \biggl(\frac{\F}{\Gk_{N-1}}\biggr)^{\!\beta}\!\cdot\ldots
                  \cdot\biggl(\frac{\F}{\Gk_0}\biggr)^{\!\beta^N}\!\!\!,
  \label{eq:product}
\end{equation}
where the index has been re-zeroed to $k_0$. The product structure reveals
the algorithm's intent: each new virtual amplitude is the target $\F$ times a
geometric-weighted product of past correction ratios $\F/\Gk_k$, with the most
recent correction weighted unity and older corrections weighted $\beta,
\beta^2,\ldots$.

A subtle but important property of \eqref{eq:product} is the symmetry of the
exponents. \emph{If} a configuration $\Gk=\F$ for all~$k$ were attainable,
the numerator and denominator exponents would sum to the same value,
$\sum_{k=0}^{N}\beta^{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\beta^{N-k}$, the product would equal
one, and we would have $\Gkp_N=\F$ --- so an exact target would be a fixed
point of the rule. The dimensional balance is what guarantees this property;
the exponent $2-\beta$ in \eqref{eq:overdrive} was chosen for exactly this
reason.

In a real kinoform problem no such configuration exists: the alternating
projections do not produce $\Gk=\F$ but instead settle at a non-zero limit
$\Gk\to\Gk_{\!\infty}\neq\F$. We retain the term ``fixed point'' below in
the dynamical-systems sense (the limit point of the iteration map), with
the understanding that this point lies at a positive distance from $\F$.

\section{Log-domain analysis}\label{sec:log}
% =====================================================================

Define the log-amplitude residuals
\begin{equation}
  u_k \;:=\; \ln\frac{\Gkp}{\F}, \qquad
  v_k \;:=\; \ln\frac{\Gk}{\F}.
\end{equation}
Taking logarithms of both sides of \eqref{eq:overdrive} yields
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;u_k \;=\; \beta\,u_{k-1} - v_k.\;}
  \label{eq:iir}
\end{equation}
This is a first-order linear difference equation: a one-pole IIR filter
applied to $-v_k$, with pole at $z=\beta$ and DC gain $-1/(1-\beta)$. Stability
of the filter alone --- ignoring the closed loop, treating $v_k$ as exogenous
input --- requires $\lvert\beta\rvert<1$, the standard pole-radius condition.

The explicit solution to \eqref{eq:iir} with $u_{-1}=0$ is
\begin{equation}
  u_N \;=\; -\!\sum_{k=0}^{N}\beta^{N-k}\,v_k,
  \label{eq:explicit}
\end{equation}
which is exactly \eqref{eq:product} after taking logarithms. The mixing
parameter $\beta$ is therefore precisely the \emph{memory time constant} of an
exponentially-weighted moving average of past log-errors, with effective time
constant $\tau = -1/\ln\beta$. The values $\beta=0.5$ and $\beta=0.9$
correspond to $\tau\approx 1.4$ and $\tau\approx 9.5$ iterations, respectively.

This recovers the qualitative ``low-pass over the iteration history''
description of \cite{Bernau2012} as a quantitative statement: the Overdrive
rule is the \emph{exponential moving average of the log-error} re-applied as a
correction to the next virtual amplitude.

\section{Mirror-descent interpretation}\label{sec:mirror}
% =====================================================================

The IIR view of Section~\ref{sec:log} is signal-processing flavor; we
now give the optimization-flavor companion. For non-negative variables on
the positive orthant, Euclidean momentum is the wrong primitive: it leaves
the orthant and amplifies wrong directions in the non-convex constraint
geometry. The right primitive is \emph{mirror descent} \cite{Beck2003,
Bubeck2015}, which performs the gradient step in the mirror coordinate
$y=\nabla\psi(x)$. For the positive orthant the canonical mirror map is
the negative entropy $\psi(x)=\sum_i x_i\ln x_i - x_i$, whose gradient is
$\nabla\psi(x)=\ln x$. Adding Polyak heavy-ball momentum in the mirror
coordinate yields
\begin{equation}
  \nabla\psi(x_{k+1}) = \nabla\psi(x_k) - \alpha\nabla f(x_k)
                          + \mu\bigl(\nabla\psi(x_k)-\nabla\psi(x_{k-1})\bigr).
\end{equation}
Specialized to the negative entropy and rewritten in $x$-coordinates,
\begin{equation}
  x_{k+1} \;=\; \frac{x_k^{\,1+\mu}}{x_{k-1}^{\,\mu}}\,e^{-\alpha\nabla f(x_k)}.
  \label{eq:mirror-heavy-ball}
\end{equation}
Identifying $x$ with the virtual amplitude $|G_{k-1}'|$, the
gradient-step factor $e^{-\alpha\nabla f}$ with the Fourier-plane
correction $|F|/|G_k|$, and $\mu$ with our $\beta$, and absorbing
constants into the $|F|^{2-\beta}$ exponent, reduces
\eqref{eq:mirror-heavy-ball} to the Overdrive rule \eqref{eq:overdrive}.
Two consequences: (i)~Overdrive is the natural heavy-ball method on
log-amplitudes, with $\beta=\mu$ and the IIR pole at $z=\beta$ identified
as the Polyak transfer-function pole. (ii)~The dimensional balance
$(2-\beta)$ is not a free choice but the $(1+\mu)/\mu$ exponent asymmetry
of the heavy-ball update.
The Bengtsson rule $|G_k'| = |G_{k-1}'|\,(|F|/|G_k|)^\beta$ corresponds in
this framing to omitting the heavy-ball asymmetry on $|G_{k-1}'|$; in log
coordinates it reads $u_k = u_{k-1} - \beta v_k$, whose
\emph{open-loop} response (treating $v_k$ as exogenous) is a discrete
integrator with pole at $z=1$. Closing the loop through the GS step
\eqref{eq:gs_linearization} below moves the pole to $z=1-\beta c$, which
is strictly inside the unit disk for $\beta,c\in(0,1)$: Bengtsson is
\emph{closed-loop stable}, just with a different pole law than Overdrive.
What changes between the two rules is therefore the pole's dependence on
$\beta$ at fixed $c$ --- Overdrive's $\beta-c$ admits an interior optimum
at $\beta=c$, while Bengtsson's $1-\beta c$ is monotone in $\beta$ and
optimized at $\beta\to 1$. The Prongu\'e variant ($\beta=1$) is the
corresponding limit and is the right specialization for the sparse
point-pattern application it was designed for.

The mirror-descent view also motivates the Lyapunov candidate of
Section~\ref{sec:lyapunov}. The Bregman divergence of the negative-entropy
mirror map is the (unnormalized) Kullback--Leibler divergence, which on
intensities is the Itakura--Saito divergence; this is the divergence
guaranteed to decrease along an exact mirror-descent step. The
log-spectral distance (LSD) used in Section~\ref{sec:lyapunov} is not this
Bregman divergence but its quadratic local approximation: a second-order
Taylor expansion of IS / KL around equality reduces to LSD up to a
constant factor. LSD is the linearization-friendly local metric induced
in log-coordinates; IS / KL is the exact multiplicative divergence the
mirror geometry distinguishes. We use LSD because our linearization is
quadratic in $\delta u_k$, but the underlying decrease property is
inherited from the mirror Bregman divergence.

\section{Closed-loop stability}\label{sec:stability}
% =====================================================================

Equation \eqref{eq:iir} treats $v_k$ as an exogenous input. In the actual
algorithm, $v_k$ is determined by the previous virtual amplitude through the
Fourier transform, hologram-plane phase-only constraint, and inverse Fourier
transform --- the standard GS step. We \emph{model} the local response of
this combined operator in log-coordinates by a scalar gain plus a stochastic
remainder,
\begin{equation}
  \delta v_k \;\approx\; c\,\delta u_{k-1} + \eta_k,
  \label{eq:gs_linearization}
\end{equation}
where $\delta u_k$ and $\delta v_k$ are perturbations around the algorithmic
limit cycle and $\eta_k$ is a zero-mean residual that captures both phase
re-shuffling and any deviation from a true scalar contraction. We make no
a-priori claim about $c\in[0,1]$ from the GS contraction proof, since that
proof bounds an $L^{2}$-amplitude contraction, not a log-amplitude scalar
gain; we treat $c$ as a free parameter and only require it to fall in the
stability range identified by the closed-loop analysis below, which is what
the numerical experiments of Section~\ref{sec:numerics} support.
Substituting \eqref{eq:gs_linearization} into \eqref{eq:iir} and noting that
the constant offset $v^*$ cancels in the linear part,
\begin{equation}
  \delta u_k \;=\; (\beta - c)\,\delta u_{k-1} - \eta_k.
  \label{eq:closed_loop}
\end{equation}
The closed-loop pole therefore sits at $z=\beta - c$. We obtain three
consequences, the first two of which the model predicts and the
experiments confirm; the third is the model's most easily falsifiable
piece and is examined in Section~\ref{sec:stochastic}.

\paragraph{Stability prediction.} The linearized iteration is stable iff
\begin{equation}
  \lvert\beta - c\rvert < 1
  \;\Longleftrightarrow\;
  c-1 < \beta < c+1.
  \label{eq:stab}
\end{equation}
For empirically observed $c\in(0,1)$, every $\beta\in(0,1)$ falls inside
\eqref{eq:stab}, which recovers the heuristic in \cite{Bernau2012} that
``$\beta$ should be chosen strictly between zero and one''. We
read this as a model-based prediction that the numerical experiments of
Section~\ref{sec:numerics} corroborate, not a theorem about the full
nonlinear iteration.

\paragraph{Locally optimal $\beta$.} The closed-loop pole $z=\beta-c$ has
minimum modulus when
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;\beta^{\,*} = c.\;}
\end{equation}
At this value the linearized response is dead-beat-like at first order. Note
that this minimizes the \emph{rate at which transient perturbations decay
back to the limit cycle}; it does not minimize the synthesis floor itself,
which is governed by Section~\ref{sec:stochastic} below. Because $c$ depends
on the operating point, $\beta^*$ is a local property of the limit cycle;
the practical implication parallels the adaptive Fienup amplitude rule of
\cite{Bernau2012}: $\beta$ should be increased toward one when the residual
is large and decreased as the iteration approaches its limit cycle.

\paragraph{Geometric transient decay.} For fixed $\beta$ and noise-free
\eqref{eq:closed_loop}, the transient decays geometrically as
$\lvert\delta u_N\rvert\sim\lvert\beta-c\rvert^N$. The driving noise $\eta_k$
prevents the residual from going to zero --- it instead settles at the floor
of Section~\ref{sec:stochastic}. We test both predictions in
Section~\ref{sec:numerics}.

\section{Lyapunov function: the log-spectral distance}\label{sec:lyapunov}
% =====================================================================

The dissertation \cite[Sec.~4.4.5]{Bernau2012} reports that the
L\textsuperscript{2} hologram-plane correction vector is generally larger than
the prior error, $\lvert x\rvert^2 > \lvert g_{\mathrm{GS}}\rvert^2$, so that
the L\textsuperscript{2} energy in the hologram plane can transiently grow
even though the algorithm converges in the image plane. This is not a
contradiction; it is a sign that the L\textsuperscript{2} hologram-plane error
is the wrong Lyapunov candidate for a multiplicative rule.

The natural candidate is the log-spectral distance (LSD)
\begin{equation}
  V_k \;:=\; \sum_{(u,v)}\biggl[\ln\frac{\lvert G_k(u,v)\rvert}{\lvert F(u,v)\rvert}\biggr]^2
       \;=\; \sum v_k^2,
  \label{eq:lyap}
\end{equation}
the local quadratic metric in log-coordinates. It is second-order
equivalent to the Itakura--Saito divergence $\mathrm{IS}(\F^2,\Gk^2)$
\cite{LeeSeung2001,Fevotte2009}, which is the Bregman divergence of the
negative-entropy mirror map of Section~\ref{sec:mirror}; LSD is the
linearization-friendly companion that matches the quadratic form of our
$\delta u$ analysis. Decomposing $v_k=v^*+\delta v_k$ around the limit
cycle, \eqref{eq:closed_loop} gives geometric transient decay of the
fluctuation component:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}\{(\delta v_{k+1})^2\}\big/\mathbb{E}\{(\delta v_k)^2\}
  \;\to\;(\beta-c)^2 \;<\; 1.
\end{equation}
The full quantity $V_k$ settles at $V_\infty\geq 0$: the constant
$\sum(v^*)^2$ plus the steady-state variance of $\delta v$. This is the
synthesis floor. The apparent paradox of a transiently growing
L\textsuperscript{2} hologram-plane correction vector is resolved:
monotonicity holds for the transient part of the LSD, the natural metric
for a multiplicative iteration.

\section{The synthesis floor: hypothesis and empirical test}\label{sec:stochastic}
% =====================================================================

If \eqref{eq:closed_loop} were an AR(1) recursion with white $\eta_k$,
squaring and taking expectations would give the textbook
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}\{(\delta u_\infty)^2\}
    \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{Var}\{\eta\}}{1 - (\beta - c)^2},
  \label{eq:ssresid}
\end{equation}
predicting a quadratic sensitivity of the floor to $\beta-c$ and a global
minimum at $\beta=c$. We tested this assumption directly: for each pixel
of each test image we ran Overdrive at $\beta=0.7$, fit AR(1) to the
$u$-trajectory in the transient window, and measured the lag-$\ell$
autocorrelation of the residuals $\hat\eta_k = u_k -
\widehat{(\beta-c)}\,u_{k-1}$. The median lag-1 of $\hat\eta$ over 10
representative images is $\approx 0.9$ (white noise would give $\approx 0$),
with lag-2 $\approx 0.6$ and lag-3 $\approx 0.3$: the innovation process is
strongly colored. A sanity check on the $v$-trajectory, which under
AR(1) with white $\eta$ should give a negative lag-1 of
$-\beta/(1+\beta^2)\approx -0.47$ for $\beta=0.7$, instead gives $+0.8$.
The closed-form floor formula \eqref{eq:ssresid} is therefore a useful
heuristic for ranking $\beta$ values --- $\beta=0.7$ does in fact yield
the best PSNR on Klasse~1 in Section~\ref{sec:numerics} --- but not a
quantitative predictor of the floor itself. A higher-order rule that
whitens the innovations could in principle do better; we tested a
two-pole extension with negligible gain. A precise model of the colored
innovations is left for future work.

\section{Numerical validation}\label{sec:numerics}
% =====================================================================

We benchmark Overdrive at three values of $\beta$ against plain GS,
Fienup--Amplitude ($\beta=0.5$), Bengtsson ($\beta=0.35$), HIO
\cite{Fienup1982} (best-tuned $\beta=0.5$, hologram constraint
$|g|=u$ replacing the support set, feedback triggered above $30\%$
deviation), RAAR \cite{Luke2005} ($\beta=0.5$), weighted GS
\cite{DiLeonardo2007}, phase-domain Polyak (MGS, $\mu=0.05$), and
Nesterov-accelerated Wirtinger Flow \cite{Candes2014} (NWF,
step $0.7$). The Klasse-1 set is 29 natural grayscale images of size
$256\times 256$, drawn from the dissertation
\cite{Bernau2012,Sheikh2005}. All algorithms share a seeded random initial
phase per image, run for 20 iterations, and apply the variant rule from
iteration~4 onward (three GS warm-up steps). We report both \emph{scaled}
PSNR (per-image total-power rescale, dissertation convention) and
\emph{unscaled} PSNR (no rescale, reflecting what a projector would
deliver).

$5.43$~dB, and Bengtsson by $9.15$~dB. The non-monotone behaviour of
Overdrive in $\beta$ (0.5\,$<$\,0.7\,$>$\,0.9) confirms the existence of an
interior optimum, qualitatively as predicted by the closed-loop pole
analysis. Note that $\beta=0.5$ has been the conventional choice motivated
by implementation simplicity (two square roots) \cite{Bernau2012}; our
results suggest the optimal value is consistently larger. The unscaled and
scaled PSNR differ by only $0.3$--$0.6$~dB across the multiplicative rules,
with unscaled values being uniformly higher: the scaled metric of
\cite{Bernau2012} therefore neither inflates nor reorders the comparison
among multiplicative IFTA variants. HIO is an exception: it loses
$\approx 7$~dB unscaled, exposing a power drift that the scaled metric
masks.

\paragraph{The alternating-projection family.} HIO at the canonical
phase-retrieval setting $\beta_{\mathrm{HIO}}=0.9$ diverges spectacularly on
grayscale kinoform synthesis because its input-plane feedback treats the
constant-amplitude constraint as an outlier set, not a support set; we
omit that row from the table. At a milder $\beta_{\mathrm{HIO}}=0.5$ HIO
ties GS on scaled PSNR but drifts $\approx 8$~dB worse on unscaled PSNR.
RAAR and WGS are stable but match GS to within $0.6$~dB and $1.9$~dB
respectively. None of these methods help: the alternating-projection
family adds memory only across constraint sets, not across iterations, and
that is not where the room for acceleration sits.

\paragraph{The additive-momentum family.} Phase-domain Polyak momentum
(MGS) underperforms GS at any positive momentum we tried; the reason is
the phase-wrap geometry, on which Euclidean momentum overshoots across
$2\pi$ jumps. NWF avoids this by operating on the complex iterate, and
is the strongest non-multiplicative method on Klasse~1 with $28.26$~dB,
$3.32$~dB above plain GS and $0.83$~dB-std the lowest per-image variance
of any method. It still lags Overdrive at $\beta=0.7$ by $4.14$~dB,
consistent with the geometric prediction.

\paragraph{Sparse targets (Klasse 2).} We repeated the benchmark on ten
point-pattern images (256$\times$256, black background, $N\in[5,50]$
Gaussian spots). Two findings, both unscaled-PSNR: \emph{(i)~RAAR's
apparent scaled-PSNR win is an artifact}. RAAR reaches scaled
$52.83$~dB but only $43.28$~dB unscaled --- worse than plain GS
($44.55$~dB) and worse than any other method tested. The $9.5$~dB
gap is power drift hidden by the per-image rescale and would not
survive on hardware. \emph{(ii)~Overdrive flattens at
$\approx 45.5$~dB across all $\beta\in\{0.5,0.7,0.9,1.0\}$ and all
warm-up lengths.} A don't-care relaxation of the zero constraint
\cite[Eq.~318]{Bernau2012} reduces full-image PSNR monotonically with
$\lambda$ and does not help either. The honest reading is that the
multiplicative-momentum machinery is geometry-specific and saturates
on sparse targets: the gradient $(|U|^2-y)U$ used by NWF has
structure on sparse $y$ that log-multiplicative updates do not
exploit, and NWF wins this regime at $47.22$~dB. Multiplicative
momentum's home is dense grayscale, where it dominates by
$\approx 4$~dB; on sparse targets it is competitive with the
classical Prongu\'e/Bengtsson family and a useful additive-momentum
method outperforms it.

\paragraph{Decay rates.} Geometric-mean per-iteration decay factors of
$V_k$ over iterations $5$--$20$ are $0.89$ (GS), $0.87$ (Fienup), $0.93$
(Bengtsson), $0.83$ (Overdrive $\beta=0.5$), $\mathbf{0.81}$
($\beta=0.7$), $0.86$ ($\beta=0.9$). The fastest decay coincides with the
highest final PSNR, in agreement with $\beta^*\approx c$.

\paragraph{Adaptive $\beta$.} Setting $\beta^*=c$ requires an estimate of
$c$. The released code ({\tt ifta.adaptive}) reads it from the geometric
decay rate of $V_k$ during the transient, with a sign disambiguation step.
On synthetic AR(1) trajectories with sufficient $|\beta-c|$ asymmetry the
estimator recovers $c$ to within $0.2$--$0.3$; near the floor it saturates
because $V_k$ ceases to decay. A fixed $\beta$ from offline calibration is
more reliable in practice.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
% =====================================================================

\paragraph{Relation to other multiplicative IFTA rules.} The Bengtsson rule
\cite{Bengtsson1998}, $\Gkp = \Gkm\,(\F/\Gk)^{\beta}$, lacks the
$\F^{\,2-\beta}/\Gk$ scaling of Overdrive and is therefore not dimensionally
balanced. In log coordinates it becomes $u_k = u_{k-1} - \beta v_k$. Treating
$v_k$ as exogenous, this open-loop response is an accumulator (open-loop pole
at $z=1$); closing the loop through the GS linearization
\eqref{eq:gs_linearization} gives a closed-loop pole at $z = 1-\beta c$, which
is in $(0,1)$ for $\beta,c\in(0,1)$. The rule is therefore stable, not
unstable: Table~\ref{tab:results} confirms it converges. Its empirical
degradation relative to GS on grayscale targets (23.25 vs.\ 24.94~dB) reflects
two effects that the linearization \emph{cannot} compare across rules: a
slower closed-loop pole at typical $\beta\!\ll\!1$, and a different noise
process $\eta_k$ at a different limit cycle. A direct numerical comparison of
the synthesis floors of Bengtsson and Overdrive, controlling for $\eta$, is
left to future work. The Prongu\'e rule \cite{Prongue1992} is the special
case $\beta=1$ of Overdrive and lies on the stability boundary; it works for
sparse point patterns but degrades on grayscale images.

\paragraph{Adjacent literatures.} Wirtinger Flow \cite{Candes2014} and
randomly initialized gradient descent \cite{Chen2018} are global
convergence guarantees for phase retrieval, where a true unknown signal
exists; they do not transfer to kinoform synthesis. Deep-learning phase
generators \cite{Zheng2021,Sun2022,Zhu2025} sidestep iteration at the cost
of training data and dedicated hardware, and answer a different question
than the one studied here. Camera-in-the-Loop GS \cite{Peng2020} addresses
the sim-to-hardware gap and reduces to GS in our sim-only benchmark; we
expect it to compose with Overdrive as a drop-in update rule.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The linearization \eqref{eq:gs_linearization}
captures only local behaviour at the limit cycle, and the colored
innovations of Section~\ref{sec:stochastic} mean \eqref{eq:ssresid} is a
ranking heuristic, not a quantitative floor predictor.

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
% =====================================================================

Kinoform synthesis is an approximation problem on the positive orthant.
Classical Euclidean momentum --- on the phase or on the complex gradient
of a Wirtinger loss --- yields modest gains; the iterate's natural geometry
is multiplicative, not Euclidean. The Overdrive rule is Polyak heavy-ball
momentum in the negative-entropy mirror geometry, with $\beta$ as the
momentum coefficient; in log-amplitudes it is a first-order IIR filter
with pole at $z=\beta$, and closing the loop predicts a closed-loop pole
at $z=\beta-c$. The log-spectral distance, locally equivalent to the
Itakura--Saito divergence of the mirror map, is the natural Lyapunov
candidate; the AR(1)-with-white-noise model that would yield a closed-form
floor is empirically refuted but qualitatively predictive. On 29 natural
grayscale images Overdrive at $\beta=0.7$ reaches $32.4$~dB after twenty
iterations, exceeding GS by $7.5$~dB and the best additive-momentum
competitor (NWF) by $4.1$~dB. On sparse point patterns the advantage of
multiplicative momentum compresses to $\approx 1$~dB --- consistent with
the classical observation that the Prongu\'e/Bengtsson family was designed
for that regime.
