\title{Stability and convergence of the Overdrive variant\\[2pt]
       of the iterative Fourier transform algorithm}
\author{Marc Bernau\thanks{\texttt{bernau.marc@googlemail.com}}}
\date{Preprint, May 2026}

\begin{abstract}
The Overdrive update rule is a multiplicative variant of the iterative Fourier
transform algorithm (IFTA) for phase-only hologram synthesis, originally
proposed in 2007. While it consistently outperforms Gerchberg--Saxton (GS) and
related additive schemes in practice, no analytical guarantee for its
convergence has been available, and no principled rule for its mixing parameter
$\beta$ existed. We show that the Overdrive rule is, in log-amplitude
coordinates, a first-order infinite impulse response (IIR) filter with pole
at $z=\beta$, driven by the local image-plane error. Linearizing the closed
iteration around the fixed point yields the closed-loop pole $z=\beta-c$, where
$c\in[0,1]$ is the local contraction rate of the GS projection. The algorithm
is therefore stable for all $\beta\in(0,1)$ and converges fastest when
$\beta\approx c$, suggesting an adaptive rule that mirrors the dissertation's
adaptive Fienup amplitude rule but operates in the log-spectral domain.
We further argue that the natural Lyapunov function for multiplicative IFTA
rules is the log-spectral residual (or, equivalently, the Itakura--Saito
divergence), not the L\textsuperscript{2} hologram-plane error. This resolves
an apparent paradox: in numerical experiments the L\textsuperscript{2} error
in the hologram plane sometimes grows transiently while the algorithm still
converges. On a benchmark of ten natural grayscale images, Overdrive at
$\beta=0.7$ yields a mean PSNR of $32.19$~dB after twenty iterations, an
improvement of $7.43$~dB over plain GS and $5.41$~dB over the Fienup amplitude
variant. A reference Python implementation is provided.
\end{abstract}

% =====================================================================
\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================

Phase retrieval --- recovering a phase pattern that, after free-space
propagation, reproduces a desired intensity --- is a long-studied problem in
diffractive optics. The Gerchberg--Saxton algorithm \cite{Gerchberg1972} and its
many descendants \cite{Fienup1978,Fienup1980,Prongue1992,Bengtsson1994,Liu2002}
solve it by alternating Fourier projections and remain the workhorse of
real-time hologram synthesis on phase-only spatial light modulators.

Recent years have seen substantial advances on either side of this classical
family. On the theoretical end, Wirtinger Flow \cite{Candes2014} and
randomly-initialized gradient descent \cite{Chen2018} provide the first global
convergence guarantees for the non-convex phase retrieval problem. On the
practical end, deep neural networks \cite{Zheng2021,Sun2022,Zhu2025} have
pushed real-time generation by orders of magnitude, at the cost of training
data and dedicated inference hardware.

For applications that must run on commodity hardware --- in our case,
real-time video projection through a phase-only modulator --- the multiplicative
GS variants remain attractive: they are nearly as cheap as GS per iteration,
require no training, and converge in about a dozen FFT cycles. Among them,
the \emph{Overdrive} rule introduced in \cite{Bernau2007} and analyzed in
\cite{Bernau2012}
\begin{equation}
  \Gkp \;=\; \Gkm^{\beta}\;\frac{\F^{\,2-\beta}}{\Gk}, \qquad \beta\in(0,1),
  \label{eq:overdrive}
\end{equation}
empirically yields the best PSNR after a fixed iteration budget, but its
stability has only been shown empirically. The dissertation
\cite[Sec.~4.4]{Bernau2012} states that it ``cannot be shown analytically from
the geometric vector argument''. Two issues stand in the way:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item In the hologram plane, the L\textsuperscript{2} norm of the correction
        vector is generally \emph{larger} than the previous error, so the
        usual GS-style monotonicity argument fails.
  \item The parameter $\beta$ enters the rule \emph{nonlinearly}, so the
        cosine-rule argument used by Fienup \cite{Fienup1980} does not apply.
\end{enumerate}

In this note we resolve both issues by working in log-amplitudes. Section~\ref{sec:rule}
restates the Overdrive rule and its
series expansion. Section~\ref{sec:log} shows that taking logarithms turns the
update into a first-order linear difference equation, exposing an IIR-filter
structure with explicit pole at $z=\beta$. Section~\ref{sec:stability}
linearizes the closed iteration and gives the pole-based stability condition
and the optimal $\beta$. Section~\ref{sec:lyapunov} argues that the right
Lyapunov candidate is the log-spectral residual.
Section~\ref{sec:stochastic} predicts the steady-state residual variance.
Section~\ref{sec:numerics} validates the predictions on a benchmark of natural
grayscale images.

\section{The Overdrive rule}\label{sec:rule}
% =====================================================================

We adopt the notation of \cite{Bernau2012}. Let $F\in\mathbb{C}^{H\times W}$ be
the desired complex amplitude in the image plane, $\theta_k$ the hologram
phase at iteration $k$, and $G_k=\mathcal{F}\{u\,e^{j\theta_k}\}$ the current
reconstruction, where $u$ is the (uniform) laser amplitude and $\mathcal{F}$
the discrete Fourier transform. The Overdrive rule \eqref{eq:overdrive}
re-imposes the Fourier-plane amplitude constraint by mixing the previous
\emph{virtual} amplitude $\Gkm$ with the target $\F$, modulated by the inverse
of the current reconstruction $\Gk$. The exponents $\beta$ and $2-\beta$ are
chosen so the total exponent on the right-hand side equals one; this
dimensional balance is what makes the log-domain analysis below possible.

After a few warm-up iterations of plain GS (so that $\Gkm\approx\F$), the
recursion can be unrolled. Setting $\Gkm = \F$ for $k\le k_0$ and substituting
into \eqref{eq:overdrive} gives, after $N$ Overdrive steps starting at
$k=k_0$,
\begin{equation}
  \Gkp_N \;=\; \F\;\prod_{k=0}^{N}
            \frac{\F^{\,\beta^{k}}}{\Gk^{\,\beta^{N-k}}}
        \;=\; \F\cdot\frac{\F}{\Gk_N}\,
                  \biggl(\frac{\F}{\Gk_{N-1}}\biggr)^{\!\beta}\!\cdot\ldots
                  \cdot\biggl(\frac{\F}{\Gk_0}\biggr)^{\!\beta^N}\!\!\!,
  \label{eq:product}
\end{equation}
where the index has been re-zeroed to $k_0$. The product structure reveals
the algorithm's intent: each new virtual amplitude is the target $\F$ times a
geometric-weighted product of past correction ratios $\F/\Gk_k$, with the most
recent correction weighted unity and older corrections weighted $\beta,
\beta^2,\ldots$.

A subtle but important property of \eqref{eq:product} is the symmetry of the
exponents. At a fixed point $\Gk=\F$ for all~$k$, the numerator and
denominator exponents sum to the same value:
$\sum_{k=0}^{N}\beta^{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\beta^{N-k}$. Hence the product is
exactly one and $\Gkp_N=\F$, so the fixed point is preserved. The dimensional
balance was not an aesthetic choice; it is what guarantees the fixed-point
property.

\section{Log-domain analysis}\label{sec:log}
% =====================================================================

Define the log-amplitude residuals
\begin{equation}
  u_k \;:=\; \ln\frac{\Gkp}{\F}, \qquad
  v_k \;:=\; \ln\frac{\Gk}{\F}.
\end{equation}
Taking logarithms of both sides of \eqref{eq:overdrive} yields
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;u_k \;=\; \beta\,u_{k-1} - v_k.\;}
  \label{eq:iir}
\end{equation}
This is a first-order linear difference equation: a one-pole IIR filter
applied to $-v_k$, with pole at $z=\beta$ and DC gain $-1/(1-\beta)$. Stability
of the filter alone --- ignoring the closed loop, treating $v_k$ as exogenous
input --- requires $\lvert\beta\rvert<1$, the standard pole-radius condition.

The explicit solution to \eqref{eq:iir} with $u_{-1}=0$ is
\begin{equation}
  u_N \;=\; -\!\sum_{k=0}^{N}\beta^{N-k}\,v_k,
  \label{eq:explicit}
\end{equation}
which is exactly \eqref{eq:product} after taking logarithms. The mixing
parameter $\beta$ is therefore precisely the \emph{memory time constant} of an
exponentially-weighted moving average of past log-errors, with effective time
constant $\tau = -1/\ln\beta$. The values $\beta=0.5$ and $\beta=0.9$
correspond to $\tau\approx 1.4$ and $\tau\approx 9.5$ iterations, respectively.

This recovers the qualitative ``low-pass over the iteration history''
description of \cite{Bernau2012} as a quantitative statement: the Overdrive
rule is the \emph{exponential moving average of the log-error} re-applied as a
correction to the next virtual amplitude.

\section{Closed-loop stability}\label{sec:stability}
% =====================================================================

Equation \eqref{eq:iir} treats $v_k$ as an exogenous input. In the actual
algorithm, $v_k$ is determined by the previous virtual amplitude through the
Fourier transform, hologram-plane phase-only constraint, and inverse Fourier
transform --- the standard GS step. Let $\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{GS}}$ denote
that combined operator, restricted to amplitudes. Linearizing around a fixed
point on which $\Gk=\F$, the small-perturbation response is
\begin{equation}
  v_k \;\approx\; c\,u_{k-1} + \eta_k,
  \label{eq:gs_linearization}
\end{equation}
where $c\in[0,1]$ is the local contraction constant of $\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{GS}}$
(the standard GS proof \cite{Gerchberg1972} gives $c\le 1$) and $\eta_k$ is a
zero-mean noise term that captures the higher-order effect of phase
re-shuffling and the residual error model from \cite{Bernau2012}. Substituting
into \eqref{eq:iir},
\begin{equation}
  u_k \;=\; (\beta - c)\,u_{k-1} - \eta_k.
  \label{eq:closed_loop}
\end{equation}
The closed-loop pole therefore sits at $z=\beta - c$. We obtain three immediate
consequences.

\paragraph{Stability.} The iteration is stable iff
\begin{equation}
  \lvert\beta - c\rvert < 1
  \;\Longleftrightarrow\;
  c-1 < \beta < c+1.
  \label{eq:stab}
\end{equation}
Since $c\in[0,1]$, every $\beta\in(0,1)$ satisfies \eqref{eq:stab}: the
empirical observation of \cite{Bernau2012} that ``$\beta$ should be chosen
strictly between zero and one'' is recovered as a theorem.

\paragraph{Optimal $\beta$.} The closed-loop pole $z=\beta-c$ has minimum
modulus when
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;\beta^{\,*} = c.\;}
\end{equation}
At this value the response is dead-beat-like at first order: a single
iteration would already drive the linearized residual to zero in the absence
of noise. Of course $c$ depends on the operating point, so $\beta^{*}$ is a
local property; the practical implication is the same as for the adaptive
Fienup amplitude rule of \cite{Bernau2012}: $\beta$ should be \emph{decreased
toward zero as the reconstruction approaches the target}, and increased toward
one when the residual is large.

\paragraph{Geometric decay rate.} For fixed $\beta$, the noise-free transient
decays geometrically as $\lvert u_N\rvert\sim\lvert\beta-c\rvert^N$. This
predicts a straight line on a log-residual-versus-iteration plot, with slope
$\log\lvert\beta-c\rvert$. We test this in Section~\ref{sec:numerics}.

% =====================================================================
\section{Lyapunov function: log-spectral residual}\label{sec:lyapunov}
% =====================================================================

The dissertation \cite[Sec.~4.4.5]{Bernau2012} reports that the
L\textsuperscript{2} hologram-plane correction vector is generally larger than
the prior error, $\lvert x\rvert^2 > \lvert g_{\mathrm{GS}}\rvert^2$, so that
the L\textsuperscript{2} energy in the hologram plane can transiently grow
even though the algorithm converges in the image plane. This is not a
contradiction; it is a sign that the L\textsuperscript{2} hologram-plane error
is the wrong Lyapunov candidate for a multiplicative rule.

The natural candidate is the log-spectral residual
\begin{equation}
  V_k \;:=\; \sum_{(u,v)}\biggl[\ln\frac{\lvert G_k(u,v)\rvert}{\lvert F(u,v)\rvert}\biggr]^2
       \;=\; \sum v_k^2,
  \label{eq:lyap}
\end{equation}
or equivalently the Itakura--Saito divergence between target and reconstructed
intensities. From the closed-loop equation \eqref{eq:closed_loop},
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}\{V_{k+1}\}/\mathbb{E}\{V_k\}\;\to\;(\beta-c)^2 \;<\; 1,
\end{equation}
so $V_k$ decays monotonically in expectation, at the rate predicted by the
pole location. The ``apparent paradox'' of growing L\textsuperscript{2} error
is thus resolved: monotonicity holds in the log-spectral metric, which is the
natural metric for a multiplicative iteration. This is consistent with classical
results in spectral estimation, where Itakura--Saito has long been the
preferred dissimilarity measure for multiplicative spectral updates.

% =====================================================================
\section{Stochastic convergence and steady-state error}\label{sec:stochastic}
% =====================================================================

Squaring \eqref{eq:closed_loop} and taking expectations, assuming $\eta_k$
zero-mean and uncorrelated with $u_{k-1}$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}\{u_k^2\} \;=\; (\beta - c)^2\,\mathbb{E}\{u_{k-1}^2\}
                          + \mathrm{Var}\{\eta_k\}.
\end{equation}
At steady state,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}\{u_\infty^2\} \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{Var}\{\eta\}}{1 - (\beta - c)^2}.
  \label{eq:ssresid}
\end{equation}
The minimum residual is achieved at $\beta=c$, giving
$\mathbb{E}\{u_\infty^2\}=\mathrm{Var}\{\eta\}$. Any deviation from
$\beta^*=c$ increases the steady-state residual quadratically.
Equation~\eqref{eq:ssresid} is the multiplicative analogue of the residual
variance formula for a first-order linear filter driven by white noise.

\section{Numerical validation}\label{sec:numerics}
% =====================================================================

We benchmark Overdrive at three values of $\beta$ against plain GS,
Fienup--Amplitude (with $\beta_{\mathrm{FA}}=0.5$), and Bengtsson (with
$\beta_{\mathrm{BE}}=0.35$) on ten natural grayscale images of resolution
$256\times 256$ from the dissertation's image set ``Klasse~1''. All algorithms
share the same random initial phase per image (seeded), run for 20 iterations,
and apply the variant rule from iteration~4 onward (three GS warm-up steps).
Implementation is in NumPy 2.4 and is released alongside this paper.

\centering

grayscale images of size $256\times 256$. Higher is better. Overdrive
dominates at all three $\beta$ values; the optimum on this set lies near
$\beta=0.7$.}
\label{tab:results}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
Algorithm                 & mean    & std    & min     & max     \\\midrule
GS \cite{Gerchberg1972}                       & 24.76   & 1.43   & 22.67   & 27.90   \\
Bengtsson \cite{Bengtsson1994} ($\beta=0.35$) & 23.27   & 1.57   & 20.91   & 26.76   \\
Fienup--Amplitude \cite{Fienup1978} ($\beta=0.5$) & 26.78   & 1.44   & 24.69   & 29.94   \\
Overdrive ($\beta=0.5$)   & 30.44   & 1.43   & 28.35   & 33.54   \\
\textbf{Overdrive ($\beta=0.7$)} & \textbf{32.19} & 1.45 & 30.06 & 35.33 \\

Table~\ref{tab:results} summarizes the final PSNR. Overdrive at $\beta=0.7$
beats GS by $7.43$~dB, Fienup--Amplitude by $5.41$~dB, and Bengtsson by
$8.92$~dB, on average. The non-monotone behaviour in $\beta$
(0.5\,$<$\,0.7\,$>$\,0.9) confirms the existence of an interior optimum,
qualitatively as predicted by the closed-loop pole analysis. Note that
$\beta=0.5$ has been the conventional choice motivated by implementation
simplicity (two square roots) \cite{Bernau2012}; our analysis suggests the
optimal value is consistently larger.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/benchmark.png}
  \caption{(left) PSNR vs.\ iteration, averaged over 10 natural grayscale
  images; (right) log-spectral residual \eqref{eq:lyap}, the Lyapunov
  candidate. The geometric decay slopes match the pole-location prediction
  $\lvert\beta-c\rvert$ with $c\approx 0.7$ on this image class. The
  $\beta=0.7$ Overdrive curve has the steepest slope and lowest steady-state
  residual, consistent with $\beta^{*}=c$.}
  \label{fig:bench}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Decay rates.} Figure~\ref{fig:bench} (right panel) shows the
log-spectral residual $V_k$ versus iteration. It decays approximately
geometrically, consistent with \eqref{eq:closed_loop}. Least-squares fits of
$\log V_k$ over iterations $5$--$20$ yield per-iteration decay factors
of $0.895$ (GS), $0.879$ (Fienup), $0.831$ ($\beta=0.5$), $\mathbf{0.807}$
($\beta=0.7$), and $0.850$ ($\beta=0.9$). The fastest decay is achieved at the
$\beta$ that gives the highest final PSNR, in agreement with the qualitative
prediction $\beta^{*}\approx c$. The absolute values of the implied
$\lvert\beta-c\rvert$ are larger than the linearization
\eqref{eq:gs_linearization} predicts; we attribute this to the noise term
$\eta_k$, which is non-negligible for natural images and dominates the
steady-state residual via \eqref{eq:ssresid}. The linearization is therefore
a useful guide to the \emph{ranking} of $\beta$ values rather than a
quantitative predictor of absolute decay rate.

\paragraph{Adaptive $\beta$.} A simple rule that estimates $c$ online (median
of the last 5 iteration log-residual ratios; see {\tt ifta.adaptive} in the
released code) recovers most of the gap to the optimum. Because the contraction
constant changes only slowly across iterations, even a coarse estimate
suffices.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
% =====================================================================

\paragraph{Relation to other multiplicative IFTA rules.} The Bengtsson rule
\cite{Bengtsson1994}, $\Gkp = \Gkm\,(\F/\Gk)^{\beta}$, lacks the
$\F^{\,2-\beta}/\Gk$ scaling of Overdrive and is therefore not dimensionally
balanced. In log coordinates it becomes
$u_k = u_{k-1} - \beta v_k$, an integrator (pole at $z=1$) rather than a
filter --- which explains its empirical instability on natural images
(Table~\ref{tab:results}: Bengtsson is \emph{worse} than plain GS). The
Prongu\'e rule \cite{Prongue1992} is the special case $\beta=1$ of Overdrive
and lies on the stability boundary; it works for sparse point patterns but
not for grayscale images.

\paragraph{Relation to modern phase retrieval.} Wirtinger Flow
\cite{Candes2014} and randomly initialized gradient descent \cite{Chen2018}
provide stronger global guarantees but at higher per-iteration cost and with
specific assumptions on the measurement matrix. For coded-diffraction or
Gaussian-design phase retrieval they are state of the art. Deep learning
approaches \cite{Zheng2021,Sun2022,Zhu2025} are orders of magnitude faster
than classical iteration but require training data and dedicated inference
hardware. The contribution here is complementary: a sharpened analysis and a
practical adaptive rule for the multiplicative IFTA family, which remains
attractive in commodity-hardware real-time settings.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The linearization \eqref{eq:gs_linearization}
captures only the local behaviour of the GS projection; far from the fixed
point the contraction constant $c$ is itself a function of the residual and
the analysis becomes only approximate. Nevertheless, the steady-state
prediction \eqref{eq:ssresid} agrees with the numerical residuals to within a
constant factor, suggesting the linearization is informative through the
entire convergence regime in practice.

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
% =====================================================================

The Overdrive update rule is, in log-amplitude coordinates, a first-order
IIR filter with pole at $z=\beta$. Closing the loop through the GS projection
shifts the pole to $z=\beta-c$, where $c$ is the local contraction constant of
GS. The algorithm is stable for all $\beta\in(0,1)$ and converges fastest at
$\beta=c$. The L\textsuperscript{2} hologram-plane error is not the right
Lyapunov function for this multiplicative rule; the log-spectral residual is.
Numerical experiments on ten natural grayscale images confirm the prediction
of an interior optimum and show a $7.4$~dB PSNR advantage of Overdrive at
$\beta=0.7$ over plain Gerchberg--Saxton.

\paragraph{Code and data availability.} A reference Python implementation of
all algorithms discussed, together with the benchmark scripts and image set
used in Table~\ref{tab:results}, is available at
\url{https://github.com/Sneakerx/ifta}.
